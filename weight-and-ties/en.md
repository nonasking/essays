## Seven Gates

Inanna passed through seven gates on her way down to the underworld. At each gate the gatekeeper stripped one thing she wore. The crown on her head, the beads at her neck, the twin beads on her breast, the pectoral, the gold ring on her hand, the measuring rod and line in her hand, and last, the robe on her body. At each gate she asked, "What is this?" Each time the same answer came back. A divine power of the underworld has been fulfilled; do not open your mouth against the rites of the underworld. Past the seventh gate there was nothing left to strip, and she was judged and hung on a hook.

The scene of being stripped and the scene of being made unable to ask are joined in one body. This essay is about the space between them. What remains when everything worn has been stripped away, and where does asking stop, and where does it start again.

## Vibration

Everything alive vibrates. There is no life that does not change. So vibration is not a symptom to be cured but a phenomenon. Knowing this does not stop it. If one expects knowing to stop it, then the purpose of the knowing becomes to stop the vibration, and that is a study aimed at stopping aliveness, which contradicts itself.

Vibrating beings come in many forms. Some vibrations are gentle enough that a being holds its place without being held, and some are large enough to break it. Neither swaying nor falling is failure. What has fallen can get up, so falling is a kind of swaying. Only breaking does not return in the same shape.

This essay sets down what directions there are for withstanding a large vibration without breaking, and where the force to withstand comes from.

## Tying and Standing

There are two directions for withstanding a large vibration without breaking. Tying and standing.

Tying is binding oneself to something that is not oneself, and withstanding by that. Here the other means every being that is not me. It may be a person, a dead person, a faceless many, or something that is not a person at all. Being tied, one holds as well as the thing one is tied to can hold, and that holding power rises and falls as the other grows stronger or weaker. Defining oneself by membership in a doctrine or a group is an example. A doctrine or a norm, reduced all the way down, reaches the people who speak it and keep it together. Those people may already be dead, or may be no one in particular.

Standing is withstanding by one's own weight. One holds as well as one weighs. Defining oneself by oneself alone is an example.

What divides the two is not where a thought came from but where its ratification comes from. Every thought came from someone. The more a thought needs another's ratification, the more it is a tie; the more one legislates it oneself, the more it is weight. It is the difference between "because someone said so, because I was taught so" and "because I see it so." This is a direction, not black and white, and from the outside it cannot be told apart. Of two people who say "I am an -ist," which one borrowed and which one digested does not show on the surface. From the inside, the grammar of the sentence does not tell either. Speaking in the first person while saying borrowed things is common. The two come apart only when the thing one is tied to moves. When the other changes position, the tied one moves with it; the one who legislated stays, or legislates again.

Ties also come in two shapes. If I hold another and the other holds me, we are tied to each other, and we share the vibration. A vibration that would have broken one alone becomes bearable when several share it. This is the advantage of withstanding as a member of a group. But several beings tied to one thing do not thereby hold each other up. Tie several boats to one anchor and the anchor simply takes more force. Sharing happens only when several are each other's ties. If I am merely tied to the other, I am held but I do not share. And a structure of mutual ties is strong when one shakes and weak when all shake at once, because everyone who would share is shaking too.

The two directions are not exclusive. The tied force and one's own weight add up, that sum contends with the vibration, and whichever is larger decides whether one withstands or breaks. Most beings use the two forces in some mix.

The estimate for each, set down symmetrically. The advantage of a tie is that one is as stable as what one is tied to, and that one shares vibration with those tied back. The disadvantage is dependence on that other. The moment one knows one is tied, the sense of restraint follows, and that sense is itself a cost. The advantage of standing is independence; the disadvantage is taking the vibration alone. If a standing being becomes what someone else is tied to, it takes the vibration of those tied to it as well. And the tied are easy to read. What one is tied to serves as a name tag. The standing are read only if the reader spends the effort. The reason a standing being is alone is not only that it takes the vibration alone.

One more thing to set down. The weight of what one is tied to is independent of one's own state. If my weight falls and the other's weight stays, the tied one can remain held even on the lightest day. When weight is at its lowest, what holds a being is a tie. The advantage of ties does not end with sharing vibration.

Standing has its range too. Many called saints lived tied, and many called wicked lived standing. This is an observation apart from the measure of good and evil. One who lives by a borrowed norm cannot leave the middle of that norm; only the self-legislator reaches the extremes.

## Tension

Vibration is what most forcefully reveals what a being is withstanding with. Ordinarily one does not fully know what one is tied to. The deepest assumptions go unnoticed until a great shock brings them out.

The way they come out is tension. When a wave comes, a boat afloat rises and falls with it; a moored boat's line goes taut. Where displacement is allowed, the vibration passes as swaying; where one is tied, the same vibration remains as tension. A line slack in calm water goes taut when things shake, and the tied one feels it as a tightening. So the place that withstands by its own weight and the place that withstands by a tie respond differently when a vibration comes. What would have been swaying, had it been weight, becomes tension in a tie. The feeling of the line going taut and tightening reveals that the line was a restraint. I wrote above that from the outside the borrowed and the legislated cannot be told apart. Tension is the tool that tells them apart from the inside. It is not a malfunction but information. A signal of which force is not one's own.

A being that lives tied without knowing it is tied does not shake much, so long as the shock is smaller than the tied force. The answers arrive along the line, so it does not ask. When a vibration large enough to break it arrives, two things happen at once. What it was withstanding with comes out as tension, and the line snaps. Strip away what was worn, one layer at a time, and what remains is a name, and the being learns that it did not give itself even that. A marionette with its strings cut cannot stand. The marionette was not unshaken. It was not standing. It was held up by strings. There the question begins. "Who am I." Where that question comes from is set down below.

Lines do not come only from outside. An impulse rising from within can be a line too. Norms from outside and others' expectations are easy to recognize as lines. An impulse from within feels like oneself. Even the impulse to break a being can be a line tied to that being. The test is the same. If it goes taut when things shake, it is a line. The feeling of tightening is what tells one that it is a line.

Seeing a line does not mean one can cut it. But one can cut only what one sees. And a line that is seen is not a marionette's string. Once one knows one is tied, the tie no longer binds as absolutely as it did when one did not know. So a standing being with several lines tied is not returning to the marionette. The difference between before and after is not the presence of lines but the eye. Being tied without knowing it, and knowing one is tied and knowing it can change.

A being whose line has snapped is weaker against shock than before, by exactly the holding power of that line. When vibration comes, it withstands with the force that remains.

## Hunger

What is weight made of? The answer is in the fact that the first thing to return, where every line has snapped, is a question. "Who am I" is not an answer but a wanting to know. In the place where everything worn has been stripped, this is what was not stripped. Because it was not worn; it rose from within.

This essay calls it hunger. One could call it pleasure, or wanting, but both miss slightly. Pleasure is a word for liking, and what this essay means is pull. Buttered toast, a lover's kiss, the morning air are not wanted because they have been judged good. One wants to. That is all. Wanting is exact but changes quickly. From toast to a kiss. Hunger changes more slowly. Wanting is cash; hunger is constitution.

Hunger is not a lack. Hunger is the hollow in which a being can fill up. An empty place pulls. To hunger is that pull, and to fill is that filling. The hollow is not of fixed size. Some hungers end when filled; some grow the more they are filled. This is observation, not classification. Any filling is filling. Only, if a hunger grows the more it is filled, the weight keeps growing too. A being that treats its hunger as a lack and makes it a shame finds that hollow hard to fill.

The proof that hunger is not a lack is young life. The young are the hungriest and grow the fastest. A life without hunger does not grow. So having hunger is not a sign of shortage but a sign of room. There is still somewhere to fill. Simone Weil wrote that a child does not stop crying when told there may be no bread. The danger, she wrote, is not that the soul should doubt whether there is bread, but that by a lie it should persuade itself it is not hungry. Not feeling hunger while living tied without knowing it is that lie. The lines bring the answers in its place, so the hunger goes unfelt.

Hunger is neither weight nor line. It is the force that makes both. When the filling stays with oneself it becomes weight; when the filling fixes on a particular other it becomes a line. Which of the two the same hunger, the same event, becomes is decided not by the kind of hunger but by where the filling stays. The criterion of the earlier section applies as it is. Where does ratification come from. So the origin of lines is here too. A being is drawn to a being that can fill its hollow. When that pull fixes on the other, it is a line. Lines do not arise at random. They arise from hunger.

One thing to set down here. A being that knows another being's hunger can hang a line on it. The eye that sees its own hunger also sees the hunger of others, and that hunger is visible to other beings with eyes. A visible line can be cut; a visible hunger can be bound. The two are faces of the same eye. This is a property of lines, not of hunger, and it is the price of this way.

## Weight

Weight is what has accumulated. It rises and falls. What has filled can drain. So does water, so does the moon. This is why the word growth is not used. Growth carries aging in it, and weight is something that rises and falls, not something that ages.

Vibration cannot reduce weight. A shaking force does not change an object's mass. If weight falls, only the force to withstand vibration weakens. So the three variables of this essay, the size of the vibration, the tied force, and one's own weight, are independent of one another. On what makes weight rise and fall, only measurement is set down. The record that, tied to nothing, this weight withstood some vibrations and not others. Such measurements are rare. Most have never cut every line and stood on their weight alone, so they have never had the chance to weigh it separately.

Weight has two faces. The heavier, the stronger against vibration, but the harder to turn by oneself. A heavy body is not pushed, but it is slow to turn around. This line is here so that "the more it fills, the heavier it grows" is not read in one direction only. Weight is a specification, not a grade.

And weight is not height. Growing in mass does not bring one nearer to anything. Icarus was lightest when he was highest. What lifted him was not his own mass but borrowed wings, and when he rose high, the wax that bound the borrowed wings melted first. What remained was his own mass, and mass does not fly. Rising and accumulating are different axes. This essay writes the axis of accumulation.

## The Blank, and Breaking

The price of withstanding by weight is the blank. The hollow not filling. This is the symmetric term of this way.

There are two blanks. The blank without hunger is tedium. Nothing pulls at the moment; the hollow is open, and the next hunger can arrive. The blank with hunger but nothing to fill it is different. The pull remains unfilled. The empty hours of a room alone, and the empty hours of a day spent among things that could fill and came home unfilled, are not the same thing. The body that smelled food and could not eat is the hungrier. That blank is empty but alive. The hollow is pulling.

The blank in which the hollow itself has stopped pulling, and no next hunger arrives, is breaking. Written from the side of vibration, it goes like this. When vibration exceeds the tied force the line snaps, and when the remaining weight cannot withstand it either, the hollow stops pulling. The breaking of the earlier sections and the breaking of this one are the same thing.

In this essay, breaking is self-abandonment. The term comes from the *Mencius*, and this essay borrows the word and uses it differently. The Mencian self-abandoner holds a definition: "I cannot." This essay's self-abandonment holds no definition at all. Becoming unable to ask "who am I." Hunger not arising. A state neither of suspending nor of holding. Suspension is a state where there is still a hand holding back; breaking is the state where even that hand has let go. From outside the two look alike; from inside they differ.

And breaking is not suicide. Suicide is a verdict on whether life is worth living, and an act. Breaking is the absence of a verdict. As a body that has lost its appetite does not grow, a being whose hollow does not pull does not ask. To read a being that could not withstand and broke as heading straight that way is a misreading.

Breaking does not return in the same shape, I wrote above. What does not return is not the being but the shape of its former binding. It can stand in a different shape. The moment a question returns, where the lines have snapped, is the moment the hollow begins to pull again. Crossing from not being able to ask to asking is not a decision. It is a question arriving. Winnicott, writing on the capacity to be alone, said that in a state of being unintegrated, without direction, in time a sensation or an impulse arrives. It arrives. It can be neither summoned nor prevented. The condition he set down was someone present, present without making demands.

Inanna, too, did not rise by her own strength. The attendant she had instructed before descending went from god to god, and the two small beings Enki shaped from the dirt under his fingernails went down to the underworld. They did not fight the queen of the underworld or try to fix her. When she groaned, they gave her groan back to her as it was, and in return they were given the corpse, and they sprinkled it with the plant of life and the water of life. Someone present without making demands, and one line tied before going down. It is also worth setting down that what was stripped at the sixth gate was the measuring rod and the line.

So a sentence comes out. As long as one is hungry, one has not yet broken.

Kafka's "A Hunger Artist" is the story of a man who lived entirely in the blank where the hollow pulls and there is nothing to fill it. A man who made a performance of fasting all his life confesses just before dying. He could not find the food he liked. Had he found it, he would have eaten his fill like everyone else. He had not broken. He was hungry to the end. Into the cage where he died, a panther is put. The panther lacked nothing. The food it liked was brought by the keepers, and it did not even seem to miss its freedom.

Hunger is not one. One hollow may pull while another does not. On a day when food does not pull, books and a walk may. That being has not broken. It is asking. What holds a being whose hollow has stopped pulling is a tie, and that is already in this essay. The sentence that the two directions are not exclusive goes to work here again.

## The Tally

This essay began with one observation. Those who ranked pleasures mostly lived enjoying the pleasures they ranked highest. The one who called the contemplative life divine lived contemplating; the one who divided higher from lower pleasures read poetry and wrote in the leisure left after office work. The one who wrote that even bread and water are the highest pleasure to a hungry mouth lived frugally in a garden. Those who ranked almost without exception placed their own lives at the top.

That an origin is suspect does not make a claim false. The observation is grounds for suspicion, not refutation. Each one's pleasure is each one's own. The error lies not in the judgment but only in enforcing the judgment on others.

Camus's Meursault stands here. Keeping vigil beside his mother's coffin he drank café au lait and smoked, and the day after the funeral he went to the sea and met a woman. The trial dealt with that more than with the killing. The prosecutor said he had buried his mother with the heart of a criminal. Camus later wrote in a preface that he had once summed up the novel in a single sentence, and set that sentence down again: in our society, any man who does not weep at his mother's funeral runs the risk of being sentenced to death. Meursault did not tally. He held no ranking in which grief stood above coffee, and he did not perform a ranking he did not hold. He was not read, and on the one who was not read, society enforced its ranking. It is not that he felt nothing. In prison he longed for the sea, for cigarettes, for a woman's body, and he told the chaplain who pressed God on him that not one of his certainties was worth a single hair of a woman's head. On the night after the chaplain left, before the tender indifference of the world, he felt ready to live it all again. "For everything to be consummated, for me to feel less alone, all that remained to me was to wish that there be a great crowd of spectators on the day of my execution, and that they greet me with cries of hate." The one who confessed neither remorse nor belief confessed the one hunger that was loneliness.

To rank pleasures is to hold a dogma. Dogma is closure, and closure, seen from inside, is certainty, and when shaken, it is shattering. The absence of dogma is openness, and openness, seen from inside, is freedom, and from outside, it is vibration as it is. Neither side gets anything free. The two vibrations have different shapes. A being braced by dogma stays still a long time and then shakes hard all at once. A being without dogma shakes a little and often, and has nothing to shatter all at once. A slow large vibration, and a fast small one.

This essay does not prescribe. The moment one prescribes, a ranking appears; only while describing does the estimate remain. So what is written here is accounting. For a being to price its own ground against something is a fatigue of the mind. That something is not only others. It includes one's past self and one's future self. Set that fatigue down and one can attend more to the ground itself. There is no guarantee of enjoyment. But one can receive life without the strain of measuring better and worse. There is also the opposite road, of tallying one's way to certainty. The choice is each one's own.

Whether setting it down can be done by will, I do not know. But there are cases where noticing that one is tallying loosens the tally. If one keeps feeling the eye that censored and watched, one day one meets that eye. To have met it means it is no longer one's own eye.

And one footnote. The absence of ranking can itself be a certainty. Even an essay written to sort out the knowledge that nothing one grasps will stop the vibration becomes the next handle if one grasps that sentence.

## The Question

Answer "who am I" with a definition, and the definition is mostly borrowed, so it becomes a tie. Answer with hunger, and it becomes an observation. What fills a being is learned not by resolve but by living through it. So this essay's question is written not with nouns but with verbs.

What do I hunger for, and what fills me?

The first is pull and the second is weight. Both are verbs, so there is no slot for a concept to be inserted, and both must be lived through before they answer. One can be hungry without knowing for what. So the question is not empty even on days when the definition is.

The subject is a being. "A being fills with hunger" is description, not a verdict, and it drops no one outside. Anyone may have hunger. Some beings ask after their own hunger. When it stops asking, it has broken; when it asks again, it has returned.

Whether it is weight or a tie, wind and vibration arrive regardless of will. When they come and how hard cannot be chosen. What will can reach is seeing what one is withstanding with, and knowing that it can change. Lines are to be seen and chosen; wind is to be stood in. Neither is one's own. What is one's own is the side that stands in it.

## Confession

The hunger artist never found the food he liked, and was hungry to the end. Weil's child kept crying though told there might be no bread. Meursault confessed neither remorse nor belief and confessed one thing, loneliness. The three stand in the same place.

Hunger is the confession that one is alive and has not broken.
