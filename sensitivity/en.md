# Sensitivity

<small>[nonasking](https://github.com/nonasking) · 2026-11 · [한국어판](../)</small>

<small>*This essay was written with heavy use of AI. Exactly how is disclosed at the end.*</small>

A sensitive person eats bread. They take in its smell, its texture, its shape. "The butter is different from yesterday's. It proofed less than usual. The shaping is finer than yesterday's." All that sensing and reading can be a pleasure. All that sensing and reading can be tiring. The pleasure and the fatigue both arrive before any choosing. What can be chosen comes after. Whether to say it or swallow it. If they say what came to mind in the bakery, they may be told they are being oversensitive and shown the door. They may be thanked and handed a cookie. The baker's response cannot be chosen.

A less sensitive person eats bread. "It's bread."

## An infant's senses

A newborn is sensitive to everything. Every sound is a first, every light is a first. At six months an infant can tell apart the speech sounds of a language it has never heard, and can tell one monkey's face from another. Around its first birthday it loses those sounds, and all monkey faces become the same face. The distinctions in use are kept; the ones not in use get lumped together. Dulling is not damage. It is learning.

So sensitivity is not a specification of the few but everyone's starting point. A sensitive adult is not someone who has something extra. It is someone who has dulled less.

A distinction survives by one of two roads. The ones kept in use because they gave pleasure survive, and the ones that could not be dropped without getting hurt survive. The discrimination of the gourmet and the discrimination of the alarm.

Distinctions do not only dull. Among alarms there is a branch that gets honed. A hand that remembers a burn and stays on guard draws back even from what is merely warm.

Whichever road it came by, the pattern has something in common. Whether sensitivity turns out a curse or a blessing, whether others welcome it or shut it out, does not depend on the sensitivity itself.

## Time

A sensitive person reads more of the same world. They gather more from the same day. Living the same ten years, they live a longer life.

That is why childhood stays long. A year full of firsts is intricate and long when you look back. A year with fewer firsts, looked back on, has its days clumped together without distinction, and the whole year folds into a few scenes.

Suppose two people living through the same span of time.

The first person. Their senses are reset every day. They begin each day with an infant's senses. A stimulus met for the first time is strange. The raw memory of meeting the unfamiliar piles up every day, layer on layer. Dense.

The second person. They accumulate their senses with no reset. They begin each day carrying what they felt the day before. A stimulus already met is familiar. Repeated, it becomes habit and skill. Sparse.

The first person's ten years are denser, longer, and heavier than the second's. A sensitive person is nearer the first.

## The instrument

There is an instrument whose strings are drawn taut and whose soundboard is thin. It sounds at the brush of a fingertip.

A taut string gives different sounds for minute differences, and once set trembling it trembles long. A slack string holds no pitch. A thin soundboard carries even a small tremor large. What has once sounded does not die down easily.

The same trait does other work too. An instrument that sounds at a fingertip also sounds at a passing wind. A sound that lingers lets the unwelcome sound linger too. At a single passing wind the instrument cries and tosses all night. A taut string snaps easily. A thin soundboard is the first to warp in the damp.

There are ways to make it quiet.

Thicken the board and it sounds less, to the wind and to the fingertip alike. The fineness that gave a different sound for fingertip and for fingernail goes, the creaking through the night goes, and it becomes a different instrument from before.

Loosen the strings and it does not sound in the wind. It trembles without pitch. The trembling does not go away. Only the sound and the note go. A loosened string gives no note to the fingertip either. Since it does not resonate, no fingertip can draw any note from it.

## The actor

Someone whose heart moves widely at a small stimulus becomes an actor. That reactivity is their instrument. They shake their own heart with a life read off a script, and the resonance carries to the seats.

A large amplitude leaves a large reverberation. It happens to that actor that the role does not leave after the curtain falls. Reduce the response and the reverberation shrinks. So does the resonance that reaches the seats.

The stage exchanges this price for gold.

## Ye (銳): sharp

The Korean word for sensitive, *yemin*, is written with two characters: 銳, sharp, and 敏, quick.

A thing that is all blade cuts the hand that holds it. An awl in a pocket pierces the pocket. A taut string cuts whatever soft thing passes across it.

Give the blade a handle and a house, and it can be held and worked. A scalpel has a handle. The blade of a plane is set in a heavy wooden body and shows by no more than a hair's breadth, and so it takes off a single shaving. The blade is as it was. Only something to hold the blade has come into being. Korean calls that thing a house, *jip*: *kaljip*, the knife's house, is a sheath, and *daepaetjip*, the plane's house, is its body.

The same goes for a string. A string becomes taut only when there is somewhere to tie it. That place has to bear the tautness. Old writings on instruments called this work of receiving the string and bearing it 承弦, read *seunghyeon* in Korean: bearing the string. Tie a string (絃) and there is an instrument; string a bow with its bowstring (弦) and there is a bow. In Korean both are read *hyeon*.

A blade in its house and a string tied to a body do not cut their surroundings merely by being near. The house holds the blade and the body bears the tautness. The awl, still sharp, goes into the pocket without piercing it.

## Min (敏): quick

Someone is shown a white wall and asked whether it is clean. Ninety-nine say yes, and one says there is a speck over there. That one sees the small speck first. The same eye sees geometry in the veins of a fallen leaf. They notice first the signs that something has gone off. They read the wilting leaf and the signs of a sick animal first.

In a symphony everyone praises, they hear a note out of place. The same ear hears harmony in birdsong.

## The room

The sound of an instrument is given a different name according to the room it sounds in. The same resonance is a performance in a concert hall and noise among the library stacks.

The names the instrument is given in the room are set down here.

Siren. The seat of one who reads and sounds first. When the siren reads danger and sounds, the room takes the sound in and readies itself for the danger.

Cassandra. The seat of one who read and spoke first, and was not received. No one believed the prophecy that Troy would fall. The prophecy came true. When it did, there was no city left to listen. The difference between the siren and Cassandra is reception. Sensitivity, when received, is called acuity; when not received, oversensitivity.

Lightning rod. The seat of one who reads and takes the danger in the room's place. The danger that would fall on the room is drawn to the lightning rod, and the lightning rod takes it instead of the room. With a lightning rod, the room can get past a danger without ever seeing when it came or what it was.

## Diagnoses

Among the names given in the room, some were names of illness.

Melancholia had two readings. There is an ancient text that asks why all those who became outstanding in philosophy, statecraft, poetry, and the arts are melancholics. Dürer engraved Melancholia as a winged woman, chin on her fist, compasses in hand. All that while melancholia was a disease of the humours, and long afterward it remained a diagnosis. Seeing deeply because of sensitivity and sinking because of it sat under one name. That name was open to both readings, genius and illness. Because the genius reading existed, there was a way for the sinking side, too, to be read as the price of genius.

Hysteria had one reading. Under that name sat paralyses, convulsions, and mutism for which no cause could be found, and emotion in general that had been judged excessive. A hospital in Paris put those patients before a lecture hall every week. Among the doctors came journalists, writers, and actors to watch. In 1980 the name lost its place as a diagnosis in the American classification and was scattered into several other names. Unlike melancholia, inside medicine the name was not read as a mark of talent.

The same instrument was priced differently according to the name it was filed under.

## Measurement

There are studies that have tried to measure sensitivity.

When the senses of people who report themselves sensitive are actually measured, they do not hear fainter sounds or catch fainter smells. How sensitive people said they were and the thresholds of their sense organs went separate ways. Sensitivity was not measured by the height of the threshold.

Measurement has also split sensitivity in two. The side that notices subtle things and the side that is shaken hard. In one study the two ran independently. That means many people have only one. But look at the questionnaire items that divided the two sides: on one side the items pair sensitivity with a positive evaluation ("notice and enjoy delicate scents") and on the other with a negative one ("made uncomfortable by loud noises"). Whether that line divides the amplitude of the resonance or divides the evaluation of the resonance cannot be known. The side this measurement counted as bad overlaps with one dimension of the personality tests. Neuroticism. It is hysteria's sibling.

## In closing

Sensitivity comes with a price. The instrument that sounds at a fingertip sounds at the wind too. A long life is heavy. A large amplitude leaves a large reverberation. Beside a blade with no house, the pocket is pierced. Flesh that passes across a thin, taut string is cut by the string.

The part that fetches a price is framed and hung in the room. It is given names such as acuity, sensibility, keenness.

Plutarch likened the Pythia, who spoke the oracles at Delphi, to an instrument. She should go down, he wrote, only when she is like an instrument well strung and well tuned. The oracles were carried off by those who came to ask. He tells of one occasion. A deputation had come from abroad, and the sacrificial victim gave no sign. The priests poured water on the victim until the sign came. The Pythia went down unwillingly. From her first answers her voice was harsh. She shrieked, ran for the exit, and fell. The deputation fled, and so did the priests. A few days later the Pythia died.

An instrument that sounds at the brush of a fingertip is hard to handle, needs tuning often, and cannot be left in just any room. An instrument does not choose its own specification. It does not choose the room it is placed in either. An instrument cannot walk. A person can.
