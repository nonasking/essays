## The Door

The word mentor comes from the Odyssey. In an Ithaca whose king had not come home, the old friend of the father who guided the boy Telemachus was named Mentor. But that friend was in fact Athena, who had borrowed a human shape. The goddess taught the boy with a friend's face, and the boy followed the friend.

So the original of this word is exactly the clause of the first essay. A person is not a light source but a medium. A teacher is the absolute borrowing a human face for a while, and that face is not a light but a window. Had Mentor registered himself as the sun, Telemachus would have become a dodder. The coda of the third essay left aside the business between stars of differing luminosity and time. This essay writes that business down. What does a large star give a small one, what does the small star give back, and how should the hierarchy between them stand?

## Two Propositions

Two propositions collide, and both are true.

One is the law of the second essay. Do not accept another's seat of the sun in trust, and do not entrust your own. The absolute cannot be found in a person, and the light of one's own sky must be switched on by oneself.

The other is what the first essay set down under Kohut's name. A child must idealize its parents. Candle worship is a necessary stage of development. A child who cannot yet walk does not survive without making its parents the sun. The garden of the first essay holds the physical case: the vine's seedling walks toward darkness on its seed reserves alone, and if it fails to reach a trunk, it dies as a seedling. Leaning is not an option of childhood but a condition of survival.

Put the two propositions in one sentence and they look like a contradiction. A child must for a while make its parents the sun, and the moment it makes them the permanent sun, it falls into misery. The solution lies in what is being leaned on.

Light and growth rings are different quantities. The first essay wrote that photosynthesis leaves growth rings: unreachable light converted into reachable matter. What a large star can give a small one is not light. Light is not transferable. What it can give is growth rings. A map of dead ends already walked, a list of mistakes already made, twenty years of felt knowledge of where a problem jams and why. So what the small star registers with the large one is not a light source but growth rings. What the second essay forbade was the registration of a light source, and the registration of growth rings does not break that law.

And the Jefferson of the third essay supplies the rest. He who lights his taper at mine. The child does not register the parent as a light source; it carries fire from the parent's flame to its own wick. Once the flame has caught, the child's fire burns as its own, the parent's fire is not diminished, and the parent is no longer needed as a source. Call this ignition. Ignition is not registration. Registration seats another's light in the seat of the sun in one's own sky; ignition lights the sun of one's own sky with another's fire.

The two propositions are therefore two faces of one discriminant, the one from the first essay. Leaning while one's own leaves are growing is ignition; leaning while one's own leaves wither is the dodder. The acts look the same. Being beside, leaning, basking. The only difference is whether, as time passes, one's own light is increasing. And the child passes through a period when that discriminant does not yet apply. The seedling bending toward the trunk is not the dodder's registration but the vine's, and the test works only after attachment. Does it grow upward again?

## Why Growth Rings Are Borrowed

Then what of the case where the student is brighter than the teacher? Gi Dae-seung may have been a brighter intelligence than Toegye. Toegye wrote first, and Gi, at thirty-two, sent his objections to Toegye at fifty-eight, and for eight years took down the replies. He went not for intelligence but for three other things.

First, growth rings. What Toegye had accumulated by fifty-eight was not luminosity but wood. Twenty years of knowing in the body where the problem of the Four Beginnings and Seven Emotions jams. Intelligence produces hypotheses quickly, but it does not know which hypotheses have already been tried. That is what time gives, and time has to be borrowed.

Second, tacit knowledge, the thing Polanyi named. Some knowledge is not transmitted in writing and moves only from person to person. Where to stop when reading a text, the feel for how a term is actually used in which context. However bright one is, that layer cannot be derived. It has to be absorbed. In the shu-ha-ri of the Japanese arts, shu is this: to break the form, the form must first be in the body, and breaking without form is not innovation but noise.

Third, correction. Intuition is refined only with feedback. A young genius's judgments have high variance; good ideas and bad ideas come out with the same conviction. What separates them is feedback grounded in the experience of the field, and the teacher is the fastest circuit for that feedback. Gi could not know by himself whether his objection was a good objection. Toegye's eight years of receiving and answering were that correction. To lean is not to obtain content but to be graded.

These three have a real instance each in the true, the good, and the beautiful. Toegye and Gi are one; another, Dostoevsky placed at the center of a novel. Raskolnikov was the brightest person in that room and Sonya, by the world's labels, the lowest. What he leaned on in her was not intelligence but growth rings, what only someone who has passed through suffering knows: the certainty that reads him the raising of Lazarus and tells him to go to the crossroads, kiss the ground, and confess. He was brighter than she; she was deeper than he. The clause of the second essay is demonstrated here. Stigma is not a photometer. So is the thesis of this essay: leaning aims not at luminosity but at growth rings.

The third is in a studio in Paris. In 1902 Rilke went to write a book about Rodin and later became his secretary. What he learned from Rodin was not poetry but how to see. Il faut travailler, rien que travailler. The discipline of looking at a thing to the end. With it Rilke wrote his thing-poems, left Rodin's side, and some years later became a teacher himself, writing letters to a young poet named Kappus. Letters to a Young Poet. What is the first sentence of the first letter? Nobody can advise or help you, nobody. Do not seek the answer outside; go into yourself. The man who had leaned on Rodin taught the next man not to lean. A chain of ignition: to carry the fire one received, and to take one's hand away from the one who has caught it.

Now the exact version of the saying that teaching and learning grow each other can be stated. Read symmetrically, "teacher and student learn from each other" erases the reason to lean; read as an asymmetric exchange, it sharpens it. The teacher learns from the student's light; the student learns from the teacher's growth rings. An exchange of different quantities: the exchange of standard candles from the third essay. Toegye saw the blind spot in his theory in Gi's objection, and Gi saw in Toegye's reply how far his objection held.

And here the progress of the first essay returns in the form of generations. A small tree leans on a large one in order to grow more, and for that moment it grows looking up not at the sun but at the larger tree. Toward what, at least as the large tree sees it, is nearer the sun. The large tree's angle to the sun is an estimate. The small tree inherits that estimate. Then, as it grows and finds its own angle, the discrepancy tells the large tree the error in its own. The teacher gives an angle; the student returns an error. If progress is the refinement of orientation, this is how that refinement crosses generations.

## Alone and Together

Beneath this essay's question lies an older one. What is the difference between pursuing the absolute together with others and pursuing it alone? The question is asked without deciding the answer in advance. Setting aside the grammar of better and worse, what is the difference?

Physics first. The third essay wrote that when coherent sources align their phases, a beam stands in one direction with the intensity of the square of their number. The other half of that sentence is this: a phased array gains one direction and loses the others. Outside the beam, the light cancels. A lone source is isotropic. In no direction is it as strong as the square, and in no direction is it erased. Alignment gains directionality and loses isotropy. So alone and together are not better and worse but two regimes: weakly in every direction, or strongly in one. For there to be light in a direction no one is aiming at, there must be an isotropic source. A branch of a shape never seen before is light in a direction the aligned array structurally cannot illuminate.

Forestry shows the same thing with trees. A tree grown alone is broad; a tree of the forest is tall. Without neighbors it spreads sideways, branches low, and thickens; in the forest it goes up narrowly and only up. There is no question of which is more of a tree. They are different shapes. The objection that the taller tree is nearer the sun is dismissed by the discriminant of the first essay. The sun is a hundred and fifty million kilometers away, so a tree's height makes no difference to the distance, and the forest tree grows tall not to approach the sun but to escape the shade of its neighbors. A tree standing alone has no need to be tall. The light already arrives whole.

What Hardy wrote about Ramanujan is the human version of this contrast. When the man who had filled notebooks alone in Madras arrived in Cambridge, those notebooks held true theorems, false theorems, and already-known theorems side by side with the same conviction. Hardy gave him two things: what was already known, and what was wrong. Growth rings and correction. And Hardy later wrote that if Ramanujan had been caught and tamed a little in his youth, he would have been a greater mathematician and discovered more that was new, but he would have been less of a Ramanujan, and the loss might have been greater than the gain. The discoverer of the shape admitted that refinement cuts shape. Alone gains shape; together gains precision.

Then what, exactly, is the precision that together gives? The first three essays wrote it down four times. The second essay: nothing in the visual field allows you to infer an eye. Since in one's own sky the self does not rise as a star but exists only as the condition of illumination, the error in one's own angle is not observed in one's own sky. The second essay: since no object is ever given whole in a single perspective, truth lives at the intersection of angles. The second and third: one's own luminosity cannot be measured by one's own observation, and the yardstick is always another's period. The third: stars that look in the same direction are brighter, toward it, than their sum. Error, angle, yardstick, magnification. All four are instruments of refinement. And none of the four is distance. The error here is not an error in one's distance from the sun. It is the geometric difference between the direction one believes one is facing and the direction one is actually bent. That difference is observed, in another's sky, as the position of a star, but that sky has no coordinates for the sun either. The ship alongside catches my drift; it does not tell me where land is.

To the first of these there is an objection. Is there not self-objectification? Is there not metacognition? A person can look at herself with another's eyes. True. But that is an imitation, an observatory of the other built inside one's own sky, and between that observatory and a real other there are three differences, all three of which psychology has measured. First, the observatory is built from one's own materials and inherits one's own blind spots intact. Kruger and Dunning found the reason a person lacking a skill cannot see the lack: the skill and the evaluation of the skill are the same organ. If the hand that grades is the hand that erred, the observatory that hand built errs in the same place. Second, the observatory cannot surprise. Pronin and colleagues showed that people see bias in others but not in themselves, and believe their own introspection to be accurate. An observer built by someone who cannot see her own bias cannot see that bias. Third, the observatory knows only what the self knows about itself. By Vazire's self-other knowledge asymmetry model, the self knows its inner traits better than others do, and others know its outer, evaluative traits better than the self does. Where one's angle went wrong is an outer quantity, and the observatory one built has no material with which to see it. A real other sees the me I do not know. Bakhtin called this the surplus of seeing. I cannot see my own face or my own back; only the other sees what I cannot see of myself. So self-objectification is a mirror and the other is a window. A mirror shows only what stands before it; through a window come words one did not say.

Yet a pursuer who structurally cannot meet others is not left without precision forever. Mendel is the answer. Alone in the monastery garden in Brno he planted peas; the paper he sent to the academy was ignored; only thirty-four years later was it rediscovered. And yet his experiments were precise. Because he had an other. The peas. Experiment is the error the world returns. A pursuer of empirical truth has a corrector even alone. There are also questions to which the world returns no error, and there only another person corrects. And Mendel shows one more thing. What isolation takes is not precision but the timing of selection. Solitary pursuit is read late, sometimes posthumously. Mendel's other arrived in 1900; Dickinson's was the sister who opened the drawer after her death. So one act remains for the solitary pursuer. Dispatch. Mendel sent the paper, and Dickinson bound the poems and put them in the drawer. That is why their afterglow could arrive.

The pathology of solitude is something else, and myth set two figures side by side at exactly that spot: Narcissus and Echo. Narcissus's problem was not that he was alone but that he mistook his reflection for another, and Echo could only repeat. The observatory built inside one's own sky is the Echo that Narcissus met. Echo could only repeat the last words of others, and so what Narcissus heard was his own speech. To mistake the return of one's own words for correction, to register an echo as another: that is the pathology of isolation, and someone who is alone and knows she is not being corrected does not have it. Seen from Echo's side it is a different illness: being able only to repeat others' words, the voice of a photoevaporated student. So self-love, too, divides in two. The self-love that lights the sun of one's own sky is the sovereignty of the second essay, and a condition. Only the self-love that hears its own echo as another is a disease.

## Pot and Forest

Then the difference between the pot and the forest must be written from two viewpoints separately.

From the tree's viewpoint there is no right answer, only specification. An epiphytic orchid dies if planted in the forest floor and lives only on trees. An oak goes straight up to the canopy in a forest and spreads wide when it stands alone. Some species germinate only in shade and some only on the bare ground of a clearing. Pot or forest is the plant's question, not botany's. Vazire's asymmetry works here again. What I need, I know better than others; whether I am growing well, others know better than I. Whether pot or forest is right falls under the sovereignty over one's own light. An orchid that chose the pot is not a coward but an orchid.

From the forest's viewpoint there are differences, and gains and losses on both sides. What pots gain is diversity of form and quarantine: disease does not pass between pots. What pots lose is accumulation and selection. A pot builds no leaf litter for the next generation, each generation starts from the beginning, and just as Ramanujan re-proved theorems already known, each pot rediscovers the same things. What the forest gains is soil, correction, and magnification. What it loses is contagion, shading, and uniformity. Coherence does not carry only good things. The stigma and backlash of the third essay are the physics of the forest. Large trees shade the seedlings, new species arise with difficulty where genes mix freely, and a forest of one species dies of one disease. And the limit of what the forest decides must be written down. The forest decides what survives; it does not decide what is good. In a forest of trees the tall tree receives more light because the resource competed for is literally light; what a forest of people selects is recognition and influence, and that is market price, not a title deed. Whether the orchid in the pot is nearer or farther from the sun than the oak in the forest, the forest does not know either.

On the balance, two fields reached the same answer. Lazer and Friedman showed, in a model, that for complex problems the faster information spreads through a network, the better the short-run and the worse the long-run performance, and that connectivity and performance stand in an inverted U: poorly connected systems and highly connected ones both do badly, and moderately connected ones do best. Zollman showed that there are cases in which a community of scientists reaches a reliable conclusion more often as a whole when its members know less of one another's results, and that there is a robust trade-off between reliability and speed, because when everyone shares immediately, a misleading early result contaminates the whole community and alternatives are abandoned before they are explored. There is a re-examination finding that this effect is not robust across parameters, and its conclusion is that the effect appears only for problems where the superior option is hard to tell from the data. That caveat lands squarely here. The pursuit of the absolute is, by definition, a problem in which arrival cannot be confirmed from data. The more unreachable the thing a community pursues, the more harmful is oversharing.

Fold the two answers together and you get a spaced forest: groves and corridors. How wide the corridors should be is not fixed, and that it is not fixed is the conclusion of both studies. Too wide is stasis, too narrow is contagion, and where between them depends on how hard the problem is.

The balance also has a form in time. Learn in the forest, go out to the pot to put out one's own branches, return to the forest to hand back the error. The rest of this essay concerns the technique of that cycle. The pathology lies on neither side but in permanence. The permanent pot is stasis; the permanent forest is uniformity. The lightless life of the first essay was the physical case of that stasis. It was not merely without light; it was the only species in its ecosystem, and what was observed was stasis. That it stood still in isolation is observation; what the researchers held most likely as the cause was not isolation but the fidelity of DNA replication and repair, and even that is a hypothesis. There is an observed stasis, and the cause is unsettled.

## Ash and Star

What a person leaves is of two kinds: products, such as writing, programs, and buildings, and persons, such as students and children. What is the difference?

A product has no self. So there are three things it cannot do. It cannot grant allegiance, it cannot return an error, and once ignited it cannot leave its author. A book carries knowledge, but it does not grade whether my objection is a good one. Mencius studied Confucius at a distance, but received no correction from him. So a medium carries growth rings, and a person carries correction and tacit knowledge.

In the language of stars: a product is ash. The heavy elements a dead star scattered, growth rings, wood. Real, lasting, the material of the next star, but not itself burning. A person is a star. Once fusion begins it burns with its own light and compresses other clouds in turn. Ash remains and stars burn. Only the ignition of another with a self is a path to receiving one's errors back, and whether that other is a child or a student, the category is the same. One can ignite without giving birth.

Diotima holds the original of this distinction. In the Symposium she divides those who are pregnant in body from those pregnant in soul, and says that those pregnant in soul beget wisdom and virtue beside a beautiful person. Homer's poems and Solon's laws are such children, she says. She gathered poems, laws, and what is begotten in a beautiful person all under the children of the soul. This essay inherits that lineage but cuts elsewhere: by the presence or absence of a self. Poems and laws are ash; what is lit inside a beautiful person is a star.

One clause on the grammar of begetting. Begetting is a byproduct of the pursuit of the absolute. A large star does not radiate in order to beget the next; radiation leaks out, and the compression of a cloud is a byproduct. That a child makes its parents better is not the child's job either. Yet there is a mechanism by which it happens. Gi Dae-seung chose to write an objection; a child returns the error in the parent's angle bodily, without choosing. Simply living differently from the parent is a correction of the parent's estimate. A product does not refute its author; another with a self refutes by merely existing. To beget is to gain an occasion for one's errors to be returned. An occasion, not an obligation. Whether that occasion shortens the distance to the sun, no one knows.

## The Teacher's Hand

The definition of a teacher lies not in teaching but in the moment of letting go.

Education calls this scaffolding, the term Wood, Bruner, and Ross gave it. An adult supports a task a child cannot do alone, but the heart of the concept is not the support but the dismantling. Scaffolding is taken down when the building stands; left up, what stands is not a building but a thing propped on scaffolding. The shu-ha-ri of the Japanese arts built departure into the definition: keep, break, leave. An apprentice who does not leave is unfinished, and a master who does not let one leave is not a master. Kram's research on mentoring counted four phases: initiation, cultivation, separation, redefinition. Separation is a normal phase. Liszt took no fees from his students all his life; hundreds left in their own styles, and there is no narrative of rivalry. Jefferson's candle walking around as a person.

The pathology is one sentence too: not letting go. The dark case in the apprenticeship literature is the butchers' apprentices, whose masters monopolized the central work so that the apprentices stayed forever at the edge. Teachers who grant no access. The apprentice version of the frozen-power theorem of the third essay.

The same studio held two answers. The year after Rilke left Rodin, Brancusi came. Rodin wanted him as an assistant, and he left after a month or so. The words attributed to him are these: nothing grows in the shade of great trees. Rilke stayed and absorbed; Brancusi left at once; both became masters. One studio gives both the clause on leaning and the clause on departure. And the clause on departure connects to the underground clause of the third essay. Complete fusion is mutual shading. When the large tree begins to shade the small one, the small one must leave the shade.

On the teacher's side, someone recited this clause himself. In the last chapter of the first part of Zarathustra he says to his disciples: now I bid you lose me and find yourselves; and only when you have all denied me will I return to you. Linji's instruction to kill the Buddha, written from the teacher's side. If the genealogy of the first essay told the student to kill the Buddha, this sentence is the Buddha telling the student to kill him.

Here care and education part. What a large tree gives a small one travels by two routes. One is downward: nutrients sent through the roots when the small tree is weak. That is not teaching but nurture, under the jurisdiction of the third essay's underground clause. The other is upward: the small tree growing while looking up at the large one, using it for a while as a proxy for the sun. That is teaching. Nutrients go down; the angle goes up. And in both the pathology is the same: care sliding into doing the work for the other. Help is help when given in the range where the other will soon manage alone. Under care that reaches past that range and does the work instead, one's own leaves find it hard to grow. That is how looking after someone becomes dependence. Care is not doing the work for someone but giving the work back. Looking after and overreach come from the same hand; they divide on whether the decision is left or taken.

## The Student's Hand

On the student's side there are three motions too.

First, to register growth rings and not a light source. Exactly the discriminant of the two-propositions section. To carry fire from the teacher's flame to one's own wick, without seating the teacher in the seat of the sun in one's own sky. The moment the teacher is seated there, the teacher ceases to be a teacher and becomes a candle.

Second, to grow upward again after attaching. The vine's test from the first essay. A foothold is for stepping on, not for living on; Brancusi's leaving after a month or so and Rilke's leaving a few years later are both this motion. When one leaves is not fixed, but that one leaves is.

Third, to grant allegiance. This the teacher cannot demand; it comes only from the student's side. Pressure without respect produces subjugation; pressure with respect produces obedience. Obedience gives up one's decision while keeping one's standing; subjugation gives up the standing too. And there is a third layer. Mencius wrote that when people are subdued by force it is not submission of the heart but only a lack of strength, and that when they are subdued by virtue they are pleased in their hearts and truly submit. Allegiance cannot be demanded from above; it is issued only from below. As Arendt distinguished authority from coercion, the moment force is used, authority has already failed. Whoever presses in order to be followed moves further from being followed the harder he presses. Subjugation obtained by force holds no recognition, so even when obtained it leaves one hungry, and on the day the force suffices, the subjugation simply vanishes; the heart was never there. So the condition on which a hierarchy remains a signpost is not the absence of pressure but the preservation of the jurisdiction of the one pressed, and the judge of whether that condition was kept is the hand below.

## The Generations of Stars

The first essay said that among people, a being is a star. Then the time axis of this essay is the generations of stars, and astronomy has the mechanism, literally. Its very name is ignition.

A large star emits ultraviolet light. That light ionizes the surrounding gas, and the layer of gas pushed by the ionization front grows heavy and collapses, and a new star is born. This is the sequential star formation that Elmegreen and Lada formalized, and the Pillars of Creation that Hubble photographed are its site. In some of the knots at the tips of the pillars, stars are igniting, and what carved those pillars is the light of the large star beside them. A large star begets a small one with light. Of the same kind. And the star so begotten grows up to do the same.

Every clause of this essay is inside this mechanism.

First, optimal frustration. If the large star's radiation is too strong, the cloud is evaporated instead of compressed, and it disperses into the large star's light without gathering into a star of its own. Photoevaporation. If the radiation is too weak, there is no compression. Only when it is right does ignition occur. Carried over into persons, photoevaporation is identification. When a teacher treats the student as an extension of himself, the student cannot gather into a star of her own and disperses into the teacher's light. What is bad for the student's growth is not the teacher's size but identification.

Second, departure. The stars born do not fall into the orbit of the large star that begot them. They spread out into loose groups. Not staying in the shade.

Third, growth rings. When a large star dies it explodes as a supernova and scatters the heavy elements it made inside itself. From that ash the next generation of stars is made. So later stars have a higher metal content than earlier ones. Astronomy divides them into populations: the old, poor stars, and the rich stars made of their ash. The sun is the latter, a star born through the ash of many generations. That the carbon and iron in our bodies were made inside dead stars is this story. Blue surpassing indigo, measured in elemental abundance. The later is made from what the earlier made, and is richer than the earlier. If the third essay set down the proverbs of the decline of the generations, cosmology says the opposite: the further down the generations, the richer the star.

Fourth, learning from the dead. A star born from a supernova remnant never met the star that made it. Born from a dead teacher's ash. That is how Mencius learned from Confucius, and how everyone who reaches the trunk through a book learns. That, too, is leaning: on a dead teacher. There is no path without leaning; one can only choose whose growth rings to lean on.

Forestry has this procedure as a work order. The method is called shelterwood. At the cut, a few overstory trees are left. Beneath them, seedlings of the same species germinate, and the partial shade of the retained trees shields them from frost and drought while they establish. The cut at that stage has a name: the seed cut. And once the seedlings are established, the retained trees are felled. That last cut has a name too: the final cut. If they are not felled, the seedlings are suppressed in the shade and do not grow. The teacher's moment of departure is written into the work order. A forest suppressed beneath trees that did not step back fails to regenerate.

One honest caveat. How large a share triggered formation holds in the birth of stars overall is under debate; there is a view that spontaneous collapse is the main mode and triggering a part. There is an observed mechanism, and its share is unsettled.

## The Vienna Triangle, a Fourth Reading

The triangle that the first essay autopsied and the third re-read as an arrangement, this essay reads as a failure of ignition.

The student's hand first. Tausk registered a light source, not growth rings. Instead of borrowing his teacher's growth rings to light his own fire, he hung the registry of his own light on Freud's signature, did not grow upward after attaching, and could not leave. The teacher's hand. Freud refused to be a foothold. He refused the analysis, delegated it, and cut it off. Not a teacher who let go but a teacher who never extended a hand. Ignition failed on both sides. One did not seek a flame to catch from; the other did not give his fire.

And there is Salomé's fourth chapter. In 1897 she met Rilke, aged twenty-one. She was thirty-six. It was she who changed his name from René to Rainer, taught him Russian, took him on two journeys to Russia, and in 1901 ended the relationship. She let him go. From then until his death, letters passed between them. The teacher who made the Rilke who would later become Rodin's secretary was she, and her hand met all three conditions of the teacher's hand: there was a term, she did not hide that she was not the light source, and she sent him back. And Rilke performed all three motions of the student's hand: he caught fire from her, he left, and a few years later he took his hand away from Kappus exactly as it had been taken from him. The woman who was the case of mismatch in the first essay and the chaser of a lost light source and the demonstration of flourishing together in the third is, in the fourth, the teacher who let go. And that student is the youngest in the genealogy of the first essay.

## Afterglow

Light arrives across light-years after the star has died. What a person leaves is like that.

There is a sentence that anyone who makes things says at least once to what they have made. Outlive me. Stay, and carry me on. Or, if the one who sees you does not see me through you, then live anyway and be a meaning to whoever meets you. Grammatically this sentence speaks to the product, but in meaning it faces a future person. Words handed to a person by way of a product. That is the place where products enter this essay: not as the partner of a relation but as its channel. A dead teacher's book is the medium that carries the relation between teacher and me beyond the teacher's absence, not a separate relation between me and the book. What the reader relates to is not the book but the person who passed through it.

And there is a symmetry. The motion by which a teacher takes her hand from a student and the motion by which an author takes her hand from a manuscript are the same. The moment of publication, interpretation belongs to the reader, and the author can no longer guard the text. To publish is to let go. Teacher and author stand under the same ethics.

Now the diagram closes on four. Upward, one pursues; inward, one lights; sideways, one aligns; downward, one carries the fire on. Downward is the direction of roots and the direction ash settles. Leaves fall downward to become soil, the ash of a large star is scattered downward to become the next star, and a teacher's fire is carried downward to catch. Light one's own wick from the fire received, carry the lit fire to the next wick, and when it has caught, let go.
